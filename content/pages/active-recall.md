# Part 4 · Active Recall, Done Right

> From Justin Sung's [ultimate guide](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eNu-pEHvZd4).

**Retrieval** is pulling knowledge *out* of memory (as opposed to *encoding*,
putting it in). Any technique built around retrieval — "active recall" — tends to
give beginners the most bang for buck, and it's the best place to start improving
because the learning curve is low and results come in hours to days.

But not all active recall is equal. There are three kinds, and one is a trap.

## The three types

1. **Free recall** — retrieve with no prompt (e.g. "tell me everything about this
   topic"). The most powerful, because it lets you recall in an *integrated*,
   elaborate way.
2. **Cued recall** — retrieve from a prompt (a flashcard question). Effective, but
   risky if you over-expose the same cue (see below).
3. **Recognition** — you see the answer and think "yeah, I knew that." **Mostly
   useless and dangerously common.**

## The recognition trap

Recognition is far easier than recall — you can recognize your country's coin
instantly but probably can't draw it from memory. So when you "test" yourself by
reading a question, glancing at the answer, and deciding it *makes sense*, you've
proven nothing about whether you could *produce* it. Many wasted study hours hide
here.

> **The fix:** always genuinely try to generate the full answer — written or
> spoken — *before* checking. If you only recognized it, mark it wrong.

## The cue-saturation trap

Repeat the same flashcard enough times and you start answering from the *first few
words* of the question rather than the knowledge itself. Then the exam phrases it
differently and you blank. **Fix:** keep switching up your cues and prompts so
you're authentically retrieving, not pattern-matching the card.

## Free recall + integration: teach it

A great free-recall technique is **teaching the topic to a 10-year-old** (the
"Feynman" approach). Teaching forces you to: structure it yourself, compare and
order ideas, and *simplify* — which means judging what's important. That builds an
integrated schema. (A plain "brain dump" is good too, but can stay isolated —
teaching forces deeper evaluation.)

## Practice how you play

Match your retrieval to how you'll actually *use* the knowledge. Three flavors of
knowledge must all be trained:

- **Declarative** — knowing *what* (the concepts, the "why").
- **Procedural** — knowing *how* to execute (writing the bug-free code, solving
  the equation).
- **Conditional** — knowing *when and why* to use which approach.

If your exam makes you solve problems, practice solving problems. If you'll code,
code. Don't only practice the part that's comfortable — and note that for many
"procedural" subjects (like maths), the under-trained *declarative* part is the
high-yield area.

## The loop

1. Learn a small chunk (encode it well).
2. Close everything and **produce** it from memory (free or varied-cue recall).
3. Check, correct, note the gaps.
4. Revisit the gaps later — at spaced intervals (see **[Spacing & the Forgetting Curve](spacing.html)**).
