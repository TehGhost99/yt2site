# Part 2 · The Effort–Time Exchange

> From Justin Sung's [how to learn faster than everyone else](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qOjSJVaBV94).

## The trade you're actually making

Most people try to become faster learners by making learning *easier* — less
effort per session. But learning happens through effortful thinking, so reducing
effort just **delays** the learning and costs you *more* time later (relearning
what didn't stick).

The real move is the opposite: **spend more effort up front to buy back time
later.** Effortful, active thinking produces stronger memory and deeper
understanding sooner — so you don't have to keep re-studying. In the research
this is the **generation effect**: actively generating answers, connections, and
structure beats passively receiving them.

## The "level of struggle" check

While you study, ask: *am I reaching the right level of struggle?*

- **Reading** passively, eyes scanning the page → no struggle → little learning.
  Instead, actively pick out which ideas matter most and how they fit together.
- **Note-taking** like a human photocopier → no struggle. Instead, decide what to
  write, how to phrase it, what's important.
- **Self-testing** by glancing then checking the answer → no struggle. Instead,
  genuinely try to produce the answer before looking.

If there's no struggle, your brain is in neutral. A bit of productive
frustration is the sound of the engine actually running.

## "How can I organize this?" — the magic question

The most powerful prompt for switching on effortful processing is:
**"How can I organize this?"** This is *not* the same as "do I understand this?"

You can understand a messy room — see everything on the floor — without it being
*organized*. Understanding without organization is shallow knowledge with weak
memory: it feels like you've learned it, but it slips away and you can't use it
to solve problems. **How long it takes you to organize something is how long it
takes you to learn it.**

In practice, organizing means: don't just read start-to-end. Pick what's
important, decide where it belongs, interrogate the source for the answers you
actually want, and rearrange ideas into something that makes sense *to you*.
That's what active reading really is.

## Why this matters for a heavy workload

When you reach the right level of struggle and organize as you go, you forget far
less — which means less time wasted relearning. Spending more effort per hour is
what makes you faster *overall*. That's the exchange.
