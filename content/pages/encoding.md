# Part 3 · Encoding: Build the Big Picture

> From Justin Sung's [ultimate guide](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eNu-pEHvZd4),
> his [study-with-me demo](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TmVsYQ4WSHc), and the
> [iteration effect](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qOjSJVaBV94).

**Encoding** is how information goes in the first time — and it's the highest-
leverage skill in learning. Encode well and your forgetting curve flattens: imagine
remembering 90% of something a week after learning it *once*. That single change
removes most of the need for endless review.

Encoding skills take longer to build than retrieval skills (weeks to months), but
they're what eventually makes learning feel effortless. Here are the principles
that make encoding effective.

## 1. Relate everything to the big picture

This is the unifying principle. For every new piece, ask: *what's the purpose of
this? Where does it fit relative to everything else?* Your brain only keeps
information it judges **relevant**, and "relevant" means *connected*. You can't
just tell your brain "this is on the exam" — it has to see how the piece fits.

## 2. Simplify first

At the moment of learning, simplify each idea until you can see how it slots into
a *simple* big picture. Find the simplest concepts, build the simplest version of
the whole first. This is **scaffolding** — you're not ready to absorb every detail
yet, and your brain would forget it anyway.

## 3. Learn in layers

Once the simple skeleton is stable, add one layer of detail, see how it fits the
big picture, then add another. In Justin's master's study demo, the first 40
pages took ~3 hours; once the structure stabilized, the next 40 took ~1 hour and
the rest just minutes — because new detail snapped onto an existing framework.

> **Organize on the way in, not after.** Like tidying a room as you go, it's far
> easier to place knowledge correctly as it arrives than to dump it and reorganize
> later (or never).

## 4. The iteration effect: guess, test, refine — fast

Don't study for weeks and only test at the end. Effective learning is a rapid
loop of **hypothesis → feedback → refine**: as each new piece arrives, guess how
it connects to what you know, then check. Wrong guesses caught early are cheap;
wrong assumptions carried for weeks are expensive to unwind. Those "lightbulb"
moments are simply a hypothesis getting confirmed.

Practical version — **test to the max** and use **micro-retrieval**: right after
learning a chunk, look away and try to reproduce/use it immediately. If you
can't, your encoding (or level of struggle) needs work — and you just got instant
feedback.

## 5. Embrace the confusion

When you first lay out a big topic it feels overwhelming. That discomfort is
cognitive load doing its job — "the confusion is good, it's fuel for growth."
Work through it by simplifying and organizing, not by writing more.

## The honest caveat

Encoding can't be reduced to one tidy trick, because most of the benefit is *how
you think*, not the tool. You could read a plain book and, by thinking this way,
encode far better than someone using a fancy app passively. The technique that helps you do it on paper is
**[Thinking on Paper](notes-on-paper.html)**.
