# Part 2 · The 6 Levels of Thinking

> From Justin Sung's [6 levels of thinking](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1xqerXscTsE)
> and his [TEDx talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TQXMl4GycD0).

Most students get stuck thinking at the wrong level — too low — and then wonder
why top results feel impossible. Here are the six levels (Bloom's revised
taxonomy) and the results each unlocks.

## The ladder

| Level | Thinking | Result you unlock |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Remember** — rote memorizing, rereading, flashcards | *Regurgitate* — list, define, state |
| 2 | **Understand** — make sense of it | *Explain* — answer "explain…" questions |
| 3 | **Apply** — use it to solve a problem | *Simple problem solving* (one concept → one problem) |
| 4 | **Analyze** — compare & contrast, find relationships | *Comparison* |
| 5 | **Evaluate** — judge, prioritize, justify ("so what?") | *Prioritize* — form & defend conclusions |
| 6 | **Create** — synthesize something new | *Hypothesize* |

Levels 1–2 are *lower-order*; 3–6 are *higher-order*. University, postgrad, and
any senior professional role test mostly at levels 4–5. Level 6 matters only at
the very highest levels, so for most people **level 5 is the target.**

## It's about thinking, not technique

Two people can do the "same" technique — say, reading a textbook or making a mind
map — while thinking at totally different levels. One rereads to cram (level 1);
the other reads to evaluate and prioritize (level 5). Same action, different
result. Intention is everything.

## Why most people won't go higher

Jumping from level 3 to 4–5 *feels harder and slower* — which (per the
[misinterpreted-effort trap](myths-mindset.html)) makes people retreat to the
easy levels. But that extra effort is your brain forming deeper, stronger memory.

## The counterintuitive method: study top-down

There are two ways to climb:

1. **Bottom-up** (memorize → understand → apply → …). Sounds logical, but it's so
   slow that most people never even reach level 5 — and worse, the lower levels
   **decay** (the forgetting curve) while you're busy with the next, so you spend
   all your time relearning.
2. **Top-down (better).** Start at **level 5 — evaluate** — and the lower levels
   fill in *automatically* as a side effect. Your brain forms memory more
   strongly at level 5 than at level 1, so aiming high gives you the lower levels
   for free. The reverse isn't true: memorizing won't make you able to evaluate.

> So when you start studying, **don't try to remember or understand.** Put your
> attention on *evaluating* — which forces you to understand and analyze along the
> way.

## Do this

- Turn study questions up the ladder: not "What is X?" but "Why does X matter?
  How does X compare to Y? Which is more important, and why?"
- Generate practice questions at levels 4–5 (an AI prompt like *"give me
  questions on [topic] at Bloom's levels 2–5"* works well) — these are exactly
  the questions exam writers use to separate good from great.
