# Part 3 · Thinking on Paper

> From Justin Sung's [think on paper](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tGrMjRqQGVY)
> and his [note-taking deep dive](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Mvxbl7Iwep4).

## What notes are actually for

Good notes do two jobs: document information, and — more importantly —
**facilitate the right thinking**. Your brain has limited capacity; notes should
*offload* the busywork (holding everything in your head) so you can spend that
freed-up capacity on deep, higher-order thinking.

The two failure modes:

- **No notes at all** — all your capacity goes to just holding information; no
  room to think.
- **Too many notes, written passively** — *even worse.* Your brain does none of
  the lifting, you bypass learning entirely, and you create a mountain of notes to
  re-process later.

> "Good note-taking should help your brain think, not help it avoid thinking."

## The three principles of thinking on paper

When new information starts to feel overwhelming, don't just transcribe it. Put
the "puzzle pieces" on the page and work them:

1. **Make it wrong.** Quickly jot keywords and guess at connections/groups —
   *it doesn't have to be right.* Perfectionism causes paralysis. The act of
   guessing primes your brain so the real learning lands faster. "If what you
   write is right, you're doing it wrong."
2. **Make it shorter.** No full sentences — get as close to single keywords as
   possible, and don't make it pretty. Research links *higher word count to lower
   retention*: summarizing into a keyword takes more thinking than copying a
   sentence, and fewer words let you spot patterns faster.
3. **Make it again.** This is where most of the power is. As you learn more, your
   map gets messy and some earlier guesses turn out wrong — so **reorganize**:
   regroup, rearrange, reconnect. The act of cleaning it up (even with no new
   information) is what strengthens the memory.

## Non-linear notes, not linear

Knowledge isn't linear, so linear (left-to-right, top-to-bottom) notes can't
represent groups and relationships well. Use **non-linear** methods — mind maps /
concept maps on an infinite canvas.

But a map only works if *you* build the relationships. Apps that auto-generate
links make it too passive — and you don't want *every* connection shown (that's
overwhelming). The thinking is in **deciding** which relationships matter: whether
to draw an arrow, how thick, which direction, where to place things.

## Make grouping intuitive *and* logical

How you group ideas matters enormously. Aim for groupings that are both:

- **Logical** — they make sense in the context of the subject, and
- **Intuitive** — obvious enough that you don't burn effort remembering the
  grouping itself. ("Outer vs. inner," "cause vs. effect," "before vs. after" are
  intuitive; an arbitrary scheme you must memorize is not.)

Making something *intuitive* helps you hold it; making it *logical* lets you
navigate it accurately. When a tricky exam question catches you out, it's often
because a grouping felt intuitive but wasn't truly logical.

## Handwriting vs. typing

As a rule of thumb, prefer **freehand** (a stylus on an infinite canvas). It's
better at expressing prioritized relationships and, being slower, forces you to
write less and think more. Typing is fine to start — but set the goal post on the
*thinking*, keep a scribble pad beside you, and move toward non-linear maps.
