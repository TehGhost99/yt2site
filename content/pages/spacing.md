# Part 4 · Spacing & the Forgetting Curve

> From Justin Sung's [ultimate guide](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eNu-pEHvZd4)
> and his [rate-limiting-step demo](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TmVsYQ4WSHc).

## The forgetting curve

In 1885, Ebbinghaus showed that after learning something once, memory drops
steeply — you might go from 90% to 10% within a day. This decay (*knowledge
decay*) hits everything eventually. But each well-timed review makes the memory
fade **more slowly** next time. That's the **spacing effect**, and it's genuinely
one of the most valuable, well-proven tools in learning.

## A simple spacing schedule

You don't need fancy software. A solid default: **review after 1 day, then 1
week, then 1 month.** The exact intervals aren't critical — 3 hours vs. a day, or
3 weeks vs. 6 weeks, all work. What matters is *consistency* of spacing, and far
more importantly, **what you do during each review** (it must be active recall
that hits an integrated schema and "practices how you play").

> The research does **not** show that a perfectly tuned interval matters much.
> The quality of the retrieval session matters far more than the algorithm.

## The flashcard trap: learning debt

Here's the limitation almost nobody warns you about. Each new flashcard commits
your *future self* to 3–7 repetitions before it sticks. One lecture might generate
30–50 cards → ~150 future reviews. Three or four lectures a day → 500–1,000
reviews stacking up *next* week, while new cards keep arriving. This is
**learning debt**, and it compounds until the system collapses under its own
weight. Very motivated students often drown in their own decks by week two.

Spacing alone is therefore **unsustainable at scale** — and it has a second
problem: answering isolated cards keeps you at low, isolated mastery, so you
struggle with complex, integrated problems no matter how many reps you do.

## Spacing is the *beginning*, not the end

Relying *only* on active recall + spaced repetition is a **rate-limiting step**:
like a barrel with one short plank, your whole learning leaks out the weakest
part. The fix is to pair spacing with strong **[encoding](encoding.html)**:

- Encode well and your forgetting curve is already flat, so you need *far fewer*
  repetitions. In Justin's 5-hour, 90-page master's session, good encoding left
  him needing only ~10–15 flashcards for the whole topic.
- Later in your studies and in professional life, almost none of your learning
  happens through flashcards. They're a supplement, not the system.

## Use flashcards well

Flashcards are great *when aligned with your goal* — best for **Reference** and
some **Evidence** information (see [PACER](pacer.html)). Keep decks small, keep
cards spaced, vary the cues, and always retrieve before flipping. If you feel
overwhelmed or can't reach higher mastery, that's the signal to invest in
encoding, not more cards.
