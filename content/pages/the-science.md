# Part 1 · How Learning Really Works

> Built from Dr. Justin Sung's [TEDx talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TQXMl4GycD0) and his [ultimate guide to remembering what you read](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eNu-pEHvZd4).

## Studying is not the same as learning

You can spend hours "studying" — reading, highlighting, rewriting notes — and
learn almost nothing. **Studying** is the activity. **Learning** is when the
knowledge is actually in your head *and you can use it.* The whole course is
about closing the gap between the two.

## The real goal: retention × mastery

To reach a serious goal (say, surviving med school), you need two things at once:

- **Retention** — holding knowledge in long-term memory long enough to use it.
- **Mastery** — the *level* of knowledge. Not just regurgitating facts, but
  applying, analyzing, and judging. (More on the levels in
  [The 6 Levels of Thinking](higher-order-thinking.html).)

There's no point having perfect recall of 300 isolated facts you can't actually
use. The aim is deep, usable knowledge that sticks.

## Isolated vs. integrated knowledge

This is the single most important idea in the course.

- **Isolated** knowledge sits in a silo — one fact, one definition, memorized on
  its own. Your brain treats isolated information as *irrelevant*, so it forgets
  it quickly. And real problems are never isolated, so siloed facts are hard to
  use.
- **Integrated** knowledge is connected — you deliberately compare ideas, find
  similarities and differences, and see how concepts relate. Your brain treats
  connected information as *relevant*, so it holds on to it.

> "Higher-order learning connects information so that your brain sees it as
> relevant and therefore worth retaining."

## Knowledge is a network (schema theory)

Your brain stores knowledge in **schemas** — networks of connected ideas. An
expert's knowledge isn't a list of facts in order; it's a web they can enter at
any point and navigate anywhere. That's what lets them solve complex problems.

Crucially: **the process you use while studying determines the quality of the
schema you build** — which then determines both your retention *and* your
mastery. Build good schemas and memory comes almost as a side effect.

There's also a **snowball effect**: the more connected knowledge you have about a
topic, the easier it is to learn more. Isolated learners get more overwhelmed as
they go; integrated learners get faster.

## Your brain rewires (neuroplasticity)

Learning is physical. With effortful, repeated practice your brain forms and
strengthens connections — *neuroplasticity*. Two practical consequences:

1. **Change takes sustained pressure.** Like building muscle at the gym, a new
   learning habit needs real, repeated effort before it becomes natural. A new
   technique feeling hard after one session doesn't mean it failed — it means
   it's not yet a habit.
2. **Your potential isn't fixed.** Most people operate at a fraction of their
   capacity. "Good memory" is mostly good *process*, and process can be trained.

## What this means for you

| The brain… | So you should… |
|---|---|
| forgets isolated info fast | connect ideas instead of memorizing facts |
| stores knowledge as schemas | build the big picture, then add detail |
| rewires with sustained effort | stick with hard new methods until they're habits |
| treats connected info as relevant | always ask "how does this relate to what I know?" |
