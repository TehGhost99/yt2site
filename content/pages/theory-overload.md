# Part 5 · Learning Skills & Mental Models

> From Justin Sung's [theory overload](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bSDprg24pEA)
> and [6 mental models](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XNzRIlcsOEQ).

So far we've focused on learning *knowledge*. Learning a *skill* (including the
skill of learning itself) has its own trap — and a couple of thinking tools help
across the board.

## The experiential cycle

You can't improve a skill by blindly repeating it. Improvement is a loop:
**experience → observe the result → decide what to change → experiment.** Fire
arrows randomly and you'll never reliably hit the target; a bad result has to tell
you *what to adjust.* Same with studying: a bad exam result means little unless
you learn what to change.

## Theory overload: the ~100% failure trap

Your brain has limited working capacity. Every new technique adds things to think
about, and an *unfamiliar* skill takes lots of capacity just to perform. Pile on
a dozen new techniques at once and you overflow — **cognitive overload** — and
learn almost nothing. A real example: a student who raced through 30% of a program
in a month actually got *worse*, while one who took a year to cover 20% improved
dramatically. The difference was overload.

> **The counterintuitive rule: to learn a skill fast, learn slower.** Only work on
> **one or two** new things at a time.

## Balance theory with practice

- **Practice with no theory** is aimless; **practice with too much theory**
  overloads you.
- As a rule of thumb, aim for **~5 hours of practice per 1 hour of new theory** —
  but adjust to *how fast you form habits*, not the clock.
- A skill becoming a **habit** frees up capacity for the next bit of theory. You
  know a habit is forming when something gets easier and faster *without* losing
  accuracy. Then — and only then — add more theory.

## Mental models (use them carefully)

Mental models are simplified frameworks for thinking and deciding. A favorite is
**expected value**: don't judge a decision as simply "good" or "bad"; weigh its
range of likely outcomes and choose what wins *on average over many repetitions*,
not just once.

But the key lesson is that **a model is only as good as how you apply it.** Feed a
model the wrong context or bad data and it confidently leads you off a cliff (a
data scientist picked the "higher-probability" email campaign from flawed past
data and lost money). So: pick the right model, question your inputs, and check
whether the situation actually fits the model's assumptions.
